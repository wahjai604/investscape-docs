# Displaying Multi-Tranche Amortization in InvestScape: Is "One Chart Per Loan with a Toggle" the Right Pattern?

## TL;DR
- **The toggle-per-tranche pattern is defensible and largely consistent with what the market actually does — but it is not the best available option.** The strongest, most standard design for InvestScape is a hybrid: keep per-tranche detail available on demand, but make the *default* a **combined view** that overlays each loan's declining balance as its own colored line on one shared time axis, plus a single **stacked "total debt service" (principal vs. interest) chart** that sums all tranches. This gives investors the "what do I owe in total, and how is it split" answer that a toggle physically cannot.
- **Almost no consumer or CRE tool combines multiple loans into a single amortization *chart* today.** Bank/lender two-mortgage tools collapse to a blended rate plus a combined schedule *table*; DealCheck and CRE models (ARGUS, PropertyMetrics) keep loans as separate per-loan records surfaced in tables. So the founder's instinct that "combining might be less clear" reflects real market behavior — but that gap is a product opportunity, not a best practice to emulate.
- **The capital-stack visualization (a stacked bar/pyramid of 1st/mezz/equity) is a separate, point-in-time composition graphic and should not be confused with amortization-over-time.** InvestScape arguably wants both: a capital-stack bar for structure and a combined balance/debt-service chart for the time dimension.

## Key Findings

**1. For a single loan, the universal standard is: a line/area chart of balance (and cumulative principal/interest) over time, PLUS a toggleable month-by-month table.** Bankrate, Zillow, NerdWallet, U.S. Bank, and calculator.net all follow this. Bankrate's chart "will display the amount of principal and interest you've paid, and your remaining loan balance, at any selected point in your amortization schedule," with a separate "Schedule" tab for the table. Zillow explicitly offers a "Chart" and a "Schedule" tab. The dominant pattern for showing the principal-vs-interest split of each payment over time is a **stacked area or stacked column chart** (the balance declines as a line; the payment composition is stacked). Microsoft's own charting documentation cites mortgage principal/interest as the textbook use case for a 100% stacked column chart.

**2. Tools that handle multiple loans overwhelmingly do NOT overlay them on one amortization chart.** Instead they use one of two reductions:
   - **Blended rate + combined totals** (Angel Oak, Lower, Griffin Funding, Omni, Churchill, dinkytown's Combination Mortgage Calculator). These collapse a 1st + 2nd/HELOC into a single weighted-average rate and a combined monthly payment. Lower's tool explicitly supports "up to 4 loan tranches," each weighted by its balance.
   - **A single combined amortization schedule (table)** listing total and individual payments (refi.com blended-rate calculator; mlsmortgage.com first-and-second calculator).
   - Where a *chart* exists on a two-phase product (calculateheloc.com), it is a balance-over-time line chart plus a principal-vs-interest stacked chart — but for a *single* loan, not multiple loans overlaid.
   - **True small-multiples or multi-series overlays of multiple loans' balances are essentially absent from mainstream mortgage tooling.** This is whitespace, not a validated anti-pattern.

**3. CRE underwriting software keeps tranches as separate per-loan records surfaced in tables — and the "capital stack" is a distinct graphic.** ARGUS Enterprise models multiple loans with a seniority ranking and a dedicated "Debt Reports → Loan Amortization" report; debt service appears as a line block on the Cash Flow report. Per the ARGUS Enterprise Training Manual (Ch. 7), the Seniority field is a "number between 1 and 30 that assigns a priority to a loan with covenants modeled… used to specify the order the loans are paid off when modeling cash sweeps. You can assign the same number to multiple loans." It is report/table-centric; there is no evidence it auto-renders a stacked debt-service chart. PropertyMetrics and typical proformas show debt service (principal + interest) as a deducted line item in the cash-flow table with year-by-year DSCR. Separately, the **capital stack** is universally drawn as a **vertical stacked bar or pyramid** showing senior debt → mezzanine → preferred equity → common equity, ordered by risk and repayment priority. Fundrise, in "The Capital Stack Explained," describes it as "frequently depicted as a bar graph or pyramid… The lower down on the stack the lower the risk to that capital, and vice versa as you move higher up." Feldman Equities describes it as "a cake with many layers." Critically, this is a **point-in-time composition** graphic (how the deal is capitalized), NOT an over-time amortization chart. The two answer different questions and both have a place.

**4. From a pure data-viz standpoint, 2–4 declining-balance series on one shared axis is well within the safe zone for a multi-series line chart.** The widely cited practical limit is **four to six series** before a "spaghetti chart" emerges — Domo's "Multi-Line Chart" guidance states: "More than five or six lines? You've got a spaghetti chart. No individual trend is readable… Limit line count to four to six series. After that, switch to small multiples or filter to a subset." Two or three loan balances sharing the same unit (dollars) and the same time axis are an ideal multi-series case, and direct end-of-line labeling (rather than a separate legend) keeps it readable. Crucially, **dual/secondary y-axes should be avoided.** Datawrapper Academy states plainly that it "does not support a chart type that is specifically designed to combine line chart and column chart… because, a lot of the times these combined charts require dual-axes — and we believe that charts with two different y-axes make it hard for most people to intuitively make right statements about two data series." That position rests on empirical work: a 2011 study cited by Datawrapper showed dual-axis graphs to 15 subjects and found "Overlap charts were inferior in both time and accuracy of reading and were rated the lowest by all but one participant… Participants reported that graphs with two scales were very confusing and required concentration and deliberation." Because all tranches share one unit (dollars), InvestScape needs only a single y-axis, which sidesteps the biggest overlay pitfall entirely.

## Details

**When is a toggle/tab actually the correct pattern, and when does it fail?**
Tabs and toggles are excellent when the user wants to *drill into one thing at a time* and does NOT need to compare across states. Their defining weakness is precisely comparison: as Onething Design notes, tabs "hide the existence of their content… a user only sees one panel's worth at a time and has to remember, or guess, what's behind the other labels." Nielsen Norman Group's work on comparison tables makes the same point — formats that force switching "do not support compensatory decision making as well, because users have to remember the attributes… in order to weigh pros and cons." A comparison-chart guide puts it plainly: side-by-side presentation lets users "weigh their options more efficiently, instead of relying on memory or flipping back and forth between tabs."

This is the crux of the InvestScape decision. If the only job-to-be-done is "show me tranche 2's schedule in isolation," the toggle is fine. But real-estate investors evaluating a leveraged deal have inherently *combined* questions: What is my total monthly debt service? When does the bridge/2nd balloon relative to the 1st? What's my total interest burden across the stack? A toggle forces the user to hold tranche 1 in working memory while looking at tranche 2 — exactly the failure mode the research warns against. The current implementation optimizes for the rarer question and makes the common one impossible.

**What "combined" should mean here — three complementary views, not one crowded chart.**
The mistake to avoid is cramming everything onto a single axis. The evidence supports a small set of purpose-built views:
   - **(a) Combined balance chart (multi-series line):** each tranche's outstanding balance as its own colored line on one shared dollar/time axis, optionally with a bolder "total debt outstanding" line. This is the single most valuable addition and is squarely within the four-to-six-series comfort zone.
   - **(b) Total debt-service composition (stacked area/column):** total principal vs. total interest per period, summed across all tranches — the direct multi-loan generalization of the standard single-loan principal/interest chart. This answers "where is my money going" for the whole deal and ties directly into DSCR, which lenders compute on *total* debt service (NOI ÷ total principal + interest).
   - **(c) Per-tranche detail (the existing toggle/table):** retained for drill-down. The toggle is genuinely the right pattern *here*, because per-tranche schedules are a drill-down, not a comparison.

**On stacked charts specifically:** a stacked area chart of *balances* across tranches is generally NOT advisable — stacked areas have a moving baseline that makes individual-series trends hard to read (Domo: "The moving baseline makes it nearly impossible to judge individual category trends for any series not at the bottom"), and balloon payoffs/refis create discontinuities that stacks handle poorly. Reserve stacking for the *debt-service (payment composition)* view, where part-to-whole is the actual question; use overlaid lines for *balances*, where trend-per-loan is the question.

**Why the market's current under-provision is an opportunity, not a warning.** The fact that Bankrate, DealCheck, and ARGUS don't overlay loan balances is largely a legacy of tooling: consumer calculators optimize for a single loan, and CRE tools are report/spreadsheet-centric. DealCheck's Help Center confirms it "allows you to add up to 5 purchase loans" (plus up to 5 refinance loans), but surfaces them as separate per-loan records in analysis and projection tables — there is no documented combined amortization chart. Their absence of a combined chart reflects the constraints and origins of those products, not a considered finding that combining is confusing. InvestScape, as a purpose-built investment platform where multi-tranche deals are a first-class scenario, is exactly the context where a combined view adds differentiated value.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Ship the combined balance overlay as the default multi-loan view (highest ROI).** When 2+ tranches exist, default to a single chart with one declining-balance line per tranche (shared single dollar y-axis, shared time x-axis), directly labeled at the end of each line, plus an optional bold "Total outstanding" line. Keep the existing per-tranche toggle available as a secondary "view detail" control. This alone resolves the founder's core concern: it makes the total-debt and relative-timing questions answerable at a glance while preserving drill-down.

**Stage 2 — Add a stacked total-debt-service chart (principal vs. interest, summed across tranches).** This mirrors the universal single-loan principal/interest visualization and connects to DSCR underwriting. Position it as a second tab alongside the balance overlay.

**Stage 3 — Add a capital-stack bar for structure (optional, distinct from amortization).** A single stacked bar showing 1st / 2nd / mezz / equity at close (and optionally at exit) gives investors the composition/seniority picture that lenders and LPs expect. Keep it clearly separate from the time-series amortization charts.

**Guardrails / thresholds that would change the recommendation:**
   - If a deal routinely carries **5+ tranches**, switch the balance view from a single overlay to **small multiples** (a grid of identically-scaled mini balance charts), per Domo's four-to-six-series spaghetti threshold.
   - **Never use a secondary y-axis** — all tranches share dollars, so one axis suffices; adding a second axis would introduce the exact misleading-scale problem the empirical literature documents.
   - If usability testing shows the overlay is misread at 3–4 tranches (e.g., users can't tell which line is which even with direct labels), fall back to small multiples rather than reverting to a toggle.
   - Retain the toggle/tab for **per-tranche detail tables** — that is a drill-down, and the toggle is the correct, standard pattern for drill-down (just not for comparison).

## Caveats
- **Direct evidence of any competitor overlaying multiple loans' balances on one amortization chart was not found** — the recommendation is grounded in general data-viz best practice plus the single-loan conventions the whole industry uses, not a competitor precedent to copy. This absence is interpreted as market whitespace, but it does mean the combined view should be validated with real users before full rollout.
- **DealCheck's exact chart rendering could not be fully confirmed** from public help docs; it is documented to support up to 5 purchase loans (and up to 5 refinance loans) as separate per-loan records surfaced in tables/projections, but whether it draws any per-loan balance chart is undocumented.
- **Several data-viz "rules" cited (four-to-six-series limit, avoid dual axes, stacked-area baseline problems) are strong professional consensus backed in places by small experimental studies, not empirical laws** — they are heuristics from practitioners (Domo, Datawrapper, Nielsen Norman Group, PolicyViz) and should be treated as well-supported defaults, not absolutes.
- **The capital stack vs. amortization distinction is definitional**: a capital-stack graphic will not answer amortization questions and vice-versa; recommending both is deliberate, not redundant.
