# Maximum Mortgage Amortization Periods in Canada and the United States (as of July 2026)

## TL;DR
- **Canada, insured (CMHC/Sagen/Canada Guaranty) mortgages:** The historic 25-year cap still applies as the default, but since **December 15, 2024**, a **30-year amortization** is available to **all first-time homebuyers** and **all buyers of newly built homes** (high-ratio, owner-occupied, property value under $1.5 million), carrying a 20-basis-point premium surcharge.
- **Canada, conventional (uninsured, 20%+ down) mortgages:** No federally mandated cap. **30 years** is the standard offering from prime lenders, with **35 years** (and occasionally up to 40 through alternative lenders) available.
- **United States:** Standard is the **30-year fixed** for conventional (Fannie/Freddie), FHA, VA, and USDA loans — all effectively cap new purchase loans at 30 years. **40-year terms exist only as loss-mitigation loan modifications**, not standard purchase products. A **50-year mortgage** was floated by the Trump administration on November 8, 2025 but remains only a proposal as of mid-2026.

## Key Findings

### Canada — Insured (high-ratio) mortgages
- Default maximum amortization remains **25 years** (imposed by federal rules in 2012).
- Effective **December 15, 2024**, a **30-year** amortization is permitted for insured mortgages where the borrower is a first-time homebuyer OR the property is newly built.
- Applies only to **high-ratio** (>80% loan-to-value), **owner-occupied** properties valued **under $1.5 million**.
- A **20-basis-point (0.20%)** premium surcharge applies to the 30-year option.

### Canada — Conventional (uninsured) mortgages
- No regulatory maximum. **30 years** is the standard prime-lender offering; **35 years** available at some lenders; up to **40 years** through alternative/subprime lenders.

### United States
- **Conventional (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac):** 30-year maximum for qualified mortgages.
- **FHA:** 30-year maximum on standard purchase loans (15- and 30-year terms offered).
- **VA:** 30-year (technically 30 years and 32 days) maximum.
- **USDA:** 30-year fixed only for Section 502 Guaranteed loans.
- **40-year:** exists only as a loss-mitigation modification tool, not a purchase product.
- **50-year:** proposal only, not implemented.

## Details

### (1) Canada — CMHC-insured maximum amortization

Canada's mortgage default insurers — the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC), Sagen, and Canada Guaranty — historically capped insured (high-ratio, less-than-20%-down) mortgages at **25 years**, a limit imposed in 2012. Before 2012, insured amortizations had reached 30, 35, and even 40 years; the federal government tightened rules in stages — from 40 to 35 years in 2008, to 30 in 2011, and to 25 in 2012.

This changed in stages in 2024:
- **Budget 2024 / effective August 1, 2024:** The federal government first allowed 30-year amortizations on insured mortgages, but only for **first-time homebuyers purchasing newly built homes**. CMHC introduced a premium surcharge effective that date, stating in its official notice: *"Effective August 1st, a premium surcharge of 20 basis points will be applied to qualified first-time homebuyers opting for the increased amortization of 30 years."*
- **September 16, 2024 announcement / effective December 15, 2024:** The government expanded eligibility to **all first-time homebuyers** (buying any home, new or resale) and **all buyers of newly built homes** (regardless of first-time status). The same reform package raised the insured-mortgage price cap from $1 million to $1.5 million. The Canada Gazette confirms the amendments "permitting 30-year amortizations for all first-time homebuyers and all purchasers of new builds supersede the previous amendments and are effective December 15, 2024."

CMHC implements this through a product branded **"CMHC Home Start."** Per CMHC's own materials, the product "will provide mortgage loan insurance to first-time homebuyers... with 30-year amortizations... allowing an additional five years to pay off their mortgage, thereby lowering monthly payments." Its official terms:
- **Maximum amortization: 30 years.**
- **Eligibility:** at least one borrower is a first-time homebuyer OR the property is newly built and not previously occupied for residential purposes.
- **Property value must be below $1,500,000.**
- Available only for **high-ratio loans (>80% LTV)** on **owner-occupied** property (1–2 units up to 95% LTV; 3–4 units up to 90%).
- **Debt-service limits:** GDS maximum 39% / TDS maximum 44%; minimum credit score 600 for at least one borrower.
- **Down payment structure:** 5% on the first $500,000 and 10% on the portion above.
- The **20-basis-point surcharge** applies on top of the base premium.

CMHC defines a first-time homebuyer as someone who has never owned a home, or has not occupied an owned principal residence in the current or four preceding calendar years, or has experienced a marital/common-law breakdown.

The standard **25-year** maximum still applies to all other CMHC-insured mortgages (e.g., the CMHC Purchase product). The two private insurers, **Sagen and Canada Guaranty, apply identical rules** — same eligibility, same 30-year maximum, same $1.5M cap, same 5%/10% down payment tiers, and the same 20-basis-point surcharge (Sagen's published rate chart: "an additional premium of 0.20% will apply"; Canada Guaranty's lender update: "The premium surcharge for 30-year amortization will continue to be 20 bps") — because all three operate under the same federal regulations (the Insurable Housing Loan Regulations and Eligible Mortgage Loan Regulations).

**Practical impact:** The affordability boost is real but modest. As RATESDOTCA mortgage expert Victor Tran put it, extending to 30 years is *"roughly a 3% to 5% increase in your borrowing power, really not that much at the end of the day."* A 25-year schedule builds equity faster and incurs less total interest.

### (2) Canada — Conventional (uninsured) maximum amortization

For uninsured mortgages (20%+ down payment), there is **no federally mandated maximum amortization period**. In practice:
- **30 years** is the standard maximum offered by prime lenders (banks).
- **35 years** is available at some lenders for low-ratio/uninsured borrowers.
- Up to **40 years** is available through alternative/non-prime lenders.

The 40-year insured mortgage was eliminated in 2008; today 35 years is generally the longest term a mainstream Canadian borrower can practically obtain, and only with 20%+ down.

### (3) United States — conventional and government-backed

- **Conventional (Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac):** The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Qualified Mortgage rules prohibit loan terms exceeding 30 years for qualified mortgages. Fannie Mae stopped purchasing 40-year-term loans. Standard maximum is 30 years.
- **FHA:** Per HUD rules, the maximum mortgage term "may not exceed 30 years from the date that amortization begins" for standard Title II single-family loans. FHA loans come in 15- and 30-year terms.
- **VA:** Maximum term is 30 years (technically 30 years and 32 days as of 2013). Common terms are 15 and 30 years.
- **USDA:** Section 502 Guaranteed loans are offered as 30-year fixed-rate only. (USDA Direct loans can be extended up to 33 or 38 years for very-low-income borrowers under payment-assistance provisions.)

**40-year mortgages** in the US exist almost exclusively as **loan modifications** (loss mitigation), not purchase products:
- HUD finalized a rule effective **May 8, 2023** allowing FHA servicers to modify defaulted loans to 40-year (480-month) terms, aligning FHA with Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which already offered 40-year modifications. USDA also permits 40-year modifications under certain conditions.
- 40-year purchase loans are non-qualified mortgages (non-QM), offered by a small number of portfolio/non-QM lenders, and represent well under 1% of originations.

**50-year mortgage proposal:** On **November 8, 2025**, President Trump (via a Truth Social post juxtaposing himself with FDR, who is credited with cementing the 30-year standard) and FHFA Director Bill Pulte floated a 50-year mortgage as an affordability measure. Pulte posted on X: *"Thanks to President Trump, we are indeed working on The 50 year Mortgage—a complete game changer."* As of early-to-mid 2026 it remains **only a proposal**: HUD Secretary Scott Turner said more research was needed, and Pulte subsequently signaled a shift, telling reporters the White House had "other priorities" and had "presented the president with anywhere between 30 to 50 different options" to improve affordability. The mechanism would require Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac to be authorized to purchase 50-year loans, and the Dodd-Frank Act's Ability-to-Repay/QM rules generally cap qualified mortgages at 30 years, so implementation would require regulatory and likely legislative change. For context on the appeal, Fannie Mae's own calculator shows a $300,000 home at a 5% rate with 5% down costing about **$1,530/month on a 30-year term versus roughly $1,294/month on a 50-year term** — a modest monthly reduction achieved by paying far more total interest and building equity much more slowly.

## Recommendations
- **Canadian first-time buyers or new-build purchasers with <20% down:** You can now request a 30-year insured amortization to lower monthly payments; budget for the 20-bps premium surcharge, and note the property must be valued under $1.5M and be owner-occupied. If you can accelerate payments, the 25-year option builds equity faster and saves interest — the borrowing-power gain from 30 years is only about 3–5%.
- **Canadian buyers with 20%+ down:** Ask lenders about 30- vs 35-year amortizations; longer terms improve qualification and cash flow but increase total interest. There is no regulated cap, so shop lenders if you want 35 years.
- **US buyers:** Expect a 30-year maximum for standard/agency loans across conventional, FHA, VA, and USDA. Treat 40-year products as niche non-QM options (mostly for modifications) and 50-year mortgages as not currently available.
- **Threshold to revisit:** For the US, monitor any FHFA/HUD rulemaking or Fannie/Freddie guideline change that would authorize 50-year purchase loans, or any CFPB change to the 30-year QM term cap — those would be the triggers to reassess. For Canada, watch federal budgets, which have repeatedly adjusted insured-amortization rules.

## Caveats
- The 50-year US mortgage is speculative; all references to it are to a proposal, not policy, and the administration's own commitment appeared to be wavering by late 2025/early 2026.
- Canadian uninsured (conventional) amortization limits are lender-set, not regulated, so specific maximums (30 vs 35 vs 40 years) vary by institution and borrower profile.
- Some secondary sources conflate "insured" with "uninsured" when describing the 30-year rules; the authoritative position is that the December 15, 2024 change applies to **insured/high-ratio** mortgages for first-time buyers and new-build purchasers, while uninsured borrowers already had access to 30+ year terms.
- Rules cited are current as of mid-2026; mortgage-insurance regulations in both countries can change with federal budgets or agency rulemaking.
